# A Trust Lifecycle Framework for Agentic AI

## Conditions and evidence criteria at each lifecycle stage

**FG-TIDA work item:** A.2.2 — *A trust lifecycle framework for agentic AI*

**Source:** Steven Zhao, independent researcher (individual capacity)

## 1. What A.2.2 asks for, and what this note answers

The Terms of Reference describe A.2.2 as:

> *"A study and specification of trust as a dynamic and time-bound property, covering initial trust establishment, continuous trust evaluation, trust degradation and revocation, including the conditions and evidence criteria required at each lifecycle stage for both agent-to-agent and human-to-agent interaction contexts."*

That description contains three requirements, and the third is the one most easily left implicit:

| A.2.2 requirement | What this note provides |
|---|---|
| trust as a **dynamic and time-bound** property | §3: a lifecycle with named stages, each with an entry and an exit condition |
| **initial establishment, continuous evaluation, degradation and revocation** | §3 and §4: the mechanisms by which trust is gained, re-evaluated, weakened and withdrawn |
| **conditions and evidence criteria at each lifecycle stage** | §4: an explicit evidence vocabulary applied per stage, separated into two axes that fail independently |

The claim this note makes is narrow: **a lifecycle framework is only testable if each stage names what evidence would satisfy it and what would contradict it.** A framework that lists stages without evidence criteria is a diagram, and a framework that names evidence without distinguishing its failure modes cannot tell "no evidence" from "evidence found wrong". The two are different findings with different responses, and the second is the one usually lost.

---

## 2. Problem statement

Current trust work concentrates on three separate points:

1. **Identity** — "who is it" (passports, registries, algorithm filing);
2. **Evidence** — "prove it" (audit trails, transparency, explainability);
3. **Release control** — "who lets it change" (change control, conformity assessment).

Almost no framework chains these into one unbroken lifecycle thread. The consequences are structural rather than incidental:

- an agent can be well-identified at birth and unverifiable in operation;
- it can be heavily evidenced in one phase and ungoverned in the next, with the seam invisible to a relying party;
- trust established at one stage is silently carried to a stage it was never evaluated for.

**Trust degradation is the specific gap.** Most frameworks describe how trust is established. Few specify the conditions under which it lapses, and fewer still specify what evidence a relying party needs in order to *notice* that it has lapsed. Revocation is typically treated as an event someone must deliver; in a system where standing is consulted at the moment of the act, it is a change in the record, and what matters is whether the next act consulted it.

---

## 3. The lifecycle

Five stages, each with an entry condition and an exit condition. The stage boundaries are drawn where the *evidence required* changes, not where organisational responsibility changes.

| # | Stage | Entry condition | Exit condition | What can go wrong silently |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L1** | **Registration** | an operator asserts an agent exists | the agent has a resolvable record with a named governance owner | registered for inventory purposes and reported as governed |
| **L2** | **Authorisation** | the agent is registered | the scope of authority it may act under is declared and bounded | authority asserted but not traceable to a conferral |
| **L3** | **Operation** | the agent acts | each consequential act leaves evidence of what it did and on what basis | acts executed with evidence produced after the fact |
| **L4** | **Re-evaluation** | new evidence, or a change to the reference the act was judged against | the trust state is re-derived and the previous state is superseded explicitly | the previous state persists because nobody re-derived it |
| **L5** | **Retirement** | the agent stops acting | the record is closed and the standing is terminally withdrawn, not merely unused | decommissioned in practice, still "active" on record |

Two design notes on the shape:

- **L4 is a stage, not an exception path.** Frameworks usually draw re-evaluation as an arrow returning to L2. Making it a stage with its own entry and exit conditions forces the question "what evidence triggers re-derivation, and who is accountable when it is not triggered".
- **L5 exists in the framework because it is the stage most often missing and most often consequential.** An agent that has stopped acting but remains on record as active is a standing that can still be relied upon by someone who was not there.

---

## 4. Evidence criteria: two axes that must not be merged

This is the part of the note that is offered as a substantive contribution rather than as a scaffold. At every lifecycle stage, the evidence a relying party needs answers two **different** questions, and a framework that reports one status for both will lose one of the failure modes.

### 4.1 Axis A — evidence state

| Value | Meaning | The mistake it is read as |
|---|---|---|
| `absent` | no evidence was produced | read as "not wrong" |
| `present-unverified` | an artefact exists but has not been checked | read as `verified` |
| `verified` | checked against a stated method, with the method named | read as "the content is true" |
| `contradicted` | evidence exists and conflicts with the claim | read as `absent` |
| `stale` | was true at a stated time, past its validity window | read as `verified` |

### 4.2 Axis B — validity of the authority to act

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `in-force` | the authority to act exists and is current |
| `suspended` | temporarily held |
| `revoked` | withdrawn |
| `expired` | lapsed by time |
| `unknown` | the record cannot be reached, so no finding is available |

### 4.3 Why the axes must not be collapsed

Three states are legitimate and each is destroyed by a single scalar:

- **`evidence = absent` with `authority = in-force`.** The act is permitted, and there is no way to show it was right. Reporting this as "authorisation failed" is wrong; reporting it as "approved" hides that nothing was checked.
- **`evidence = verified` with `authority = revoked`.** Good evidence of a *past* act does not confer *present* authority. This is the case that a per-action check must catch and a lifecycle record must make visible.
- **`evidence = contradicted`.** This is not a stronger form of `absent`. It is a different finding, it calls for a different response, and it is the one lost most often — because a schema with one status field has no place to put it, so it is recorded as "no evidence", which reads as a gap rather than a conflict.

**`unknown` deserves its own line.** If the record cannot be reached at the moment of decision, the correct output is that no finding is available — not that the previous state persists, and not that the check passed. An enforcement point that cannot distinguish "could not check" from "checked and fine" has no way to report the difference, and neither does anyone reading its log afterwards.

### 4.4 Criteria per stage

Applying the two axes at each lifecycle stage yields the table this note proposes as the A.2.2 answer:

| Stage | Evidence criterion (Axis A) | Authority criterion (Axis B) |
|---|---|---|
| **L1 Registration** | the record exists and is resolvable: `verified`; a claimed registration that cannot be resolved: `present-unverified` | the governance owner is named; a record with no owner is `unknown` for every downstream question |
| **L2 Authorisation** | the conferral is evidenced, not asserted: `verified` vs `present-unverified` | scope declared and bounded; an unbounded scope is not `in-force` in any usable sense |
| **L3 Operation** | per act: what was done, on what inputs, on what basis; absence of per-act evidence is `absent`, not "no incident" | the authority at the moment of the act, which may differ from the authority at the moment of queueing |
| **L4 Re-evaluation** | the triggering evidence is recorded, and the re-derived state supersedes the previous one explicitly | the pre-change and post-change states are both on record, with which one governed at which time |
| **L5 Retirement** | the closure is evidenced, distinguishing "stopped acting" from "removed from record" | terminal withdrawal, distinguishable from `suspended` |

## 5. Three laws as the lifecycle thread

The framework is organised around three executable properties, one per phase of the thread. They are stated as properties because a property can be tested; a principle cannot.

| Property | Lifecycle anchor | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Registry** | L1 → L2 | every agent has a resolvable record with a named governance owner, and the authority it acts under is traceable to a conferral — no exemption by size or private use |
| **Evidence** | L3 → L4 | every consequential act leaves evidence sufficient for a party who was not present to establish what happened and on what basis, and re-evaluation is triggered by named events rather than by discretion |
| **Gates** | L4 → L5 | capability changes and authority expansions pass an explicit gate: trigger, assessment, release, retrospective review; and retirement closes the record rather than leaving it dormant |

**Where the medical-device lifecycle does and does not transfer.**

An earlier draft of this clause called the transfer "an isomorphism". A specialist and an
adversarial review both rejected that word, and they were right: isomorphism is a claim of
structure-preserving correspondence, and the correspondence does not hold.

What does transfer is the **shape**: the regulated world already runs a sequence in which
classification precedes design control, which precedes evidence generation, which precedes
registration, which precedes post-market observation, which precedes retirement. Five stages
with per-stage evidence criteria and an explicit re-evaluation point are already operated under
regulatory obligation, and the lifecycle shape is not in question.

What does not transfer is the **relation being governed**, and this is where the earlier claim
broke. Medical-device regulation governs a *product-to-conformity* relation: the object is a
device, and the question is whether it conforms. A.2.2 governs a *mandate-to-action* relation:
the object is an agent acting under delegated authority, and the question is whether this act
was within it. The consequences of the difference are specific rather than stylistic:

- **L2 has no medical-device counterpart.** A device does not hold "the scope of authority it
  may act under"; it holds a conformity certificate. The authority belongs to the manufacturer.
  The agent, by contrast, acts, and the scope of what it may do is the central object of L2.
- **Registration differs in kind.** Registering a device registers an object in a catalogue;
  registering an agent (L1) must also establish who answers for it, because the agent will take
  actions for which someone is accountable.
- **Retirement differs in consequence.** A retired device is no longer placed on the market. A
  retired agent's standing can still be relied on by a party that was not present, which is why
  L5 exists in this framework at all.

So the transfer is of a **template, not of a relation**. The device lifecycle is offered here as
evidence that a five-stage structure with per-stage evidence criteria is operable, not as
evidence that the stages mean the same thing. A reader who wants the mapping will find it
partial, and the three gaps above are the partiality.

## 6. How this composes with A.2.7 and with WG2

This note deliberately does not propose a control-plane architecture (A.2.7), a per-action verdict model (WG2), or a record format. The division of labour the note assumes:

| Question | Where it belongs |
|---|---|
| What are the lifecycle stages and the evidence criteria at each? | **this note (A.2.2)** |
| How is trust state established, distributed and changed across an estate? | A.2.7 (control plane), as proposed by its own proposer |
| What decision does an enforcement point return for one action, and what does it record? | WG2 |
| What must a record contain for a party who was not present? | the record-requirements work (themes#1) |

Two interfaces this note asks for, stated as **requirements rather than as architecture**, so that the owner of each can accept, rewrite or reject them:

1. **Each decision records the version or recency of the record it consulted.** Without it, a later reader cannot distinguish a decision taken against current standing from one taken against superseded standing. This is indifferent to mechanism: where changes are pushed it is how *delivered* is told from *in force*; where standing is consulted at the act it is how a regulator establishes that no act was decided on superseded standing.
2. **"No record found" and "record found, standing revoked" are distinguishable outcomes.** If both present as the same denial, nothing downstream can tell a refused act from a broken check.

Both requirements are consequences of the lifecycle framework's own claim: that trust is time-bound. A framework that says trust is time-bound, and does not require the time to be recorded, has not said anything a relying party can use.

## 8. Proposed action for the Focus Group

1. **Consider this lifecycle and its evidence criteria as an input to A.2.2.** The five stages are offered as a candidate answer to "conditions and evidence criteria required at each lifecycle stage", not as a settled one.
2. **Test the two-axis vocabulary against the group's own use cases.** The specific testable claim is that any single-status representation of trust loses at least one of the three legitimate states in §4.3. If a use case can be found in which a single scalar is sufficient, that would be a useful counterexample and this note would be revised.
3. **Keep the evidence criteria separable from the architecture.** The criteria are about what a relying party needs to establish; the architecture is about how it is delivered. Specifying them together tends to produce a framework that can only be implemented one way.
4. **Consider the retirement stage explicitly.** It is the stage most often omitted and the one where an unclosed record can still be relied upon.

## 8. Proposed action for the Focus Group

1. **Consider this lifecycle and its evidence criteria as an input to A.2.2.** The five stages are offered as a candidate answer to "conditions and evidence criteria required at each lifecycle stage", not as a settled one.
2. **Test the two-axis vocabulary against the group's own use cases.** The specific testable claim is that any single-status representation of trust loses at least one of the three legitimate states in §4.3. If a use case can be found in which a single scalar is sufficient, that would be a useful counterexample and this note would be revised.
3. **Keep the evidence criteria separable from the architecture.** The criteria are about what a relying party needs to establish; the architecture is about how it is delivered. Specifying them together tends to produce a framework that can only be implemented one way.
4. **Consider the retirement stage explicitly.** It is the stage most often omitted and the one where an unclosed record can still be relied upon.
